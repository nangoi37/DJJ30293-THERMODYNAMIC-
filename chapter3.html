Chapter 3: First Law of Thermodynamics & Its Processes

3.1 The First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics, also known as the conservation of energy principle, states that energy can be neither created nor destroyed during a process; it can only change forms. This involves the study of the relationships between various forms of energy through energy interactions.[cite: 2]

Forms of Energy

Energy exists in multiple forms that convert into one another:[cite: 2]

  • Kinetic Energy: Energy of movement.[cite: 2]
  • Potential Energy: Stored energy (e.g., Gravitational, Elastic, Chemical, Nuclear).[cite: 2]
  • Thermal Energy: Energy of moving particles (heat).[cite: 2]
  • Mechanical & Electrical Energy: Energy of objects in motion and particles moving through a wire.[cite: 2]

3.2 Energy Balance of a System

The net change (increase or decrease) in the total energy of the system during a process is equal to the difference between the total energy entering and the total energy leaving the system.[cite: 2]

$$E_{\text{in}} - E_{\text{out}} = \Delta E_{\text{system}}$$

Mechanisms of Energy Transfer include:[cite: 2]

  • Heat Transfer ($Q$): $Q_{\text{in}}$ (into system) and $Q_{\text{out}}$ (out from system).[cite: 2]
  • Work Transfer ($W$): $W_{\text{in}}$ (done on system) and $W_{\text{out}}$ (done by system).[cite: 2]
  • Mass Flow ($m$): $m_{\text{in}}$ (entering system) and $m_{\text{out}}$ (leaving system).[cite: 2]
Stationary Systems:
A closed system whose velocity and elevation of the center of gravity remain constant during a process.[cite: 2]
Since $z_1 = z_2 \rightarrow \Delta PE = 0$, and $V_1 = V_2 \rightarrow \Delta KE = 0$.[cite: 2]
Therefore, $\Delta E = \Delta U$.[cite: 2]

3.3 Closed Systems (Non-Flow Processes)

Any process undergone by a closed system is referred to as a non-flow process. A closed system involves only heat transfer and work transfer, with no mass flow across the boundary. The Non-Flow Energy Equation is:[cite: 2]

$$Q_{\text{net, in}} - W_{\text{net, out}} = U_2 - U_1$$

The Five Non-Flow Processes

These processes define how an ideal gas behaves when enclosed in a piston-cylinder without mass escaping:[cite: 2]

Figure 3.1: The Five Non-Flow Thermodynamic Processes.

1. Polytropic Process ($PV^n = C$): A general process where both heat and work cross the boundary. Most actual compressions (e.g., in air compressors and IC engines) fall between isothermal and isentropic conditions and are therefore polytropic.[cite: 2]
$W = \frac{P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2}{n - 1} = \frac{mR(T_1 - T_2)}{n - 1}$
$Q = \left(\frac{\gamma - n}{\gamma - 1}\right) W$

Example: Polytropic Expansion

The combustion gases in a petrol engine are at 30 bar and 800°C before expansion. The gases expand through a volume ratio of 8.5 to occupy 510 cm³. The polytropic index n = 1.15.[cite: 2]

Solution:
Using $P_1 V_1^n = P_2 V_2^n$, we rearrange for $P_2 = P_1 (V_1/V_2)^n$.
$P_2 = 30 \times (1 / 8.5)^{1.15} = 2.58 \text{ bar}$.[cite: 2]
For temperature, $T_2 / T_1 = (V_1 / V_2)^{n-1}$.
$T_2 = 1073 \times (1 / 8.5)^{0.15} = 801 \text{ K}$ (or 528 °C).[cite: 2]
Work, $W = \frac{P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2}{n - 1}$. With $V_2 = 510 \text{ cm}^3$ ($0.00051 \text{ m}^3$) and $V_1 = 60 \text{ cm}^3$, $W = 0.528 \text{ kJ}$.[cite: 2]

2. Isothermal Process ($PV = C$): Temperature is constant ($T_1 = T_2$). This occurs when compression is carried out slowly, allowing heat to be extracted by cooling water. All heat added is converted into work; internal energy remains constant ($U_2 - U_1 = 0$).[cite: 2]
$W = Q = P_1 V_1 \ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right) = P_1 V_1 \ln\left(\frac{P_1}{P_2}\right)$[cite: 2]

Example: Isothermal Compression

0.4 kg of oxygen ($M=32 \text{ kg/kmol}$) is compressed isothermally from 1.01 bar and 220°C to 5.5 bar.[cite: 2]

Solution:
Gas constant $R = 8.3144 / 32 = 0.2598 \text{ kJ/kgK}$.
Temperature $T = 220 + 273 = 493 \text{ K}$.[cite: 2]
$W = m R T \ln(P_1/P_2) = 0.4 \times 0.2598 \times 493 \times \ln(1.01 / 5.5) = -86.7 \text{ kJ}$.[cite: 2]
Since process is isothermal, $dU = 0$, so $Q = W = -86.7 \text{ kJ}$ (Heat is rejected).[cite: 2]

3. Adiabatic Process ($PV^\gamma = C$): A thermally insulated system where negligible heat transfer occurs ($Q = 0$). For example, the outer casings of steam engines, steam turbines, and gas turbines are well insulated so fluid expansion is assumed adiabatic. This occurs if compression is very rapid.[cite: 2]
$W = \frac{P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{mR(T_1 - T_2)}{\gamma - 1}$[cite: 2]

Example: Adiabatic Compression

In an insulated air compressor, air at 0.98 bar and 20°C is compressed to 1/6 of its original volume. Mass is 0.05 kg, $\gamma = 1.4$, and $C_v = 0.718 \text{ kJ/kgK}$.[cite: 2]

Solution:
$T_2 = T_1 \times (V_1/V_2)^{\gamma-1} = 293 \times (6)^{0.4} = 600 \text{ K}$.[cite: 2]
$P_2 = P_1 \times (V_1/V_2)^\gamma = 0.98 \times (6)^{1.4} = 12.04 \text{ bar}$.[cite: 2]
$W = m C_v (T_1 - T_2) = 0.05 \times 0.718 \times (293 - 600) = -11.02 \text{ kJ}$.[cite: 2]

4. Isobaric Process ($P_1 = P_2$): Pressure remains constant. For example, heating water in a cylinder where the load/weight on the piston is kept constant.[cite: 2]
$W = P(V_2 - V_1)$ and $Q = m C_p (T_2 - T_1) = m(h_2 - h_1)$[cite: 2]

Example: Isobaric Expansion

2.25 kg of fluid expands from 0.1 m³ to 0.2 m³ at a constant 7 bar. Initial and final enthalpies are 210 kJ/kg and 280 kJ/kg.[cite: 2]

Solution:
$Q = m(h_2 - h_1) = 2.25 \times (280 - 210) = 157.5 \text{ kJ}$.[cite: 2]
$W = P(V_2 - V_1) = 700 \text{ kPa} \times (0.2 - 0.1) = 70 \text{ kJ}$.[cite: 2]
$\Delta U = Q - W = 157.5 - 70 = 87.5 \text{ kJ}$.[cite: 2]

5. Isometric/Isochoric Process ($V_1 = V_2$): Volume remains constant. For example, heating an aerosol can or a rigid walled vessel.[cite: 2]
$W = 0$ and $Q = U_2 - U_1 = m C_v (T_2 - T_1)$[cite: 2]

Example: Isochoric Heating

Specific internal energy of 2 kg of fluid increases from 120 kJ/kg to 180 kJ/kg during constant volume process. Find heat required.[cite: 2]

Solution:
Since $W = 0$, the first law is $Q = \Delta U$.[cite: 2]
$Q = m(u_2 - u_1) = 2 \times (180 - 120) = 120 \text{ kJ}$.[cite: 2]

3.4 Open Systems (Flow Processes)

In an open system, energy transfers take place across the boundary, and fluid also crosses the boundary.[cite: 2]

Equation of Continuity

If a system is in a steady state, the mass of fluid passing any section during a specified time must be constant.[cite: 2]

$$\dot{m} = \frac{A_1 C_1}{v_1} = \frac{A_2 C_2}{v_2}$$

The Steady Flow Energy Equation (SFEE)

For a steady flow, the properties of the fluid at any section must be constant with respect to time. The general equation in units of kW (kJ/s) is:[cite: 2]

$$Q - W = \dot{m} \left[ (h_2 - h_1) + \frac{C_2^2 - C_1^2}{2} + g(Z_2 - Z_1) \right]$$

3.5 Steady Flow Engineering Devices

1. Turbine

A turbine utilizes a pressure drop to produce work energy to drive an external load. Fluid passes quickly, so heat transfer is negligible ($Q=0$). Potential and kinetic energy changes are normally small enough to be neglected.[cite: 2]
SFEE reduces to: $-W = \dot{m}(h_2 - h_1)$.[cite: 2]

Figure 3.2: Control volume diagram for a Turbine.

Example: Turbine Power

Fluid flows at 45 kg/min. Specific enthalpy drop is 580 kJ/kg, and turbine loses 2100 kJ/min as heat. Determine power ($W$).[cite: 2]

Solution:
$\dot{m} = 45 / 60 = 0.75 \text{ kg/s}$.[cite: 2]
$\dot{Q}_{\text{loss}} = -2100 / 60 = -35 \text{ kW}$.[cite: 2]
$Q - W = \dot{m}(h_2 - h_1) \rightarrow -35 - W = 0.75 \times (-580)$.[cite: 2]
$W = 435 - 35 = 400 \text{ kW}$.[cite: 2]

2. Boiler

Water is pumped in and steam leaves at the same rate. Heat is supplied from a furnace. No moving parts ($W=0$). Kinetic and potential energy are neglected.[cite: 2]
SFEE reduces to: $Q = \dot{m}(h_2 - h_1)$.[cite: 2]

Example: Boiler Heat Supply

Boiler evaporates 1000 kg/h. Inlet enthalpy = 165 kJ/kg, outlet enthalpy = 2200 kJ/kg. Find heat supplied.[cite: 2]

Solution:
$Q = \dot{m}(h_2 - h_1)$.[cite: 2]
$Q = 1000 \times (2200 - 165) = 2,035,000 \text{ kJ/h}$.[cite: 2]

3. Nozzle

A nozzle utilizes a pressure drop to produce an increase in kinetic energy. Fluid spends short time inside ($Q=0$). No moving parts ($W=0$). Potential energy neglected.[cite: 2]
SFEE reduces to: $C_2 = \sqrt{2(h_1 - h_2)}$ (assuming $C_1$ is negligible).[cite: 2]

Example: Nozzle Exit Area

Fluid with $h_1 = 2800 \text{ kJ/kg}$ enters horizontally with negligible velocity at 14 kg/s. At outlet, $h_2 = 2250 \text{ kJ/kg}$ and $v_2 = 1.25 \text{ m}^3/\text{kg}$. Determine outlet area.[cite: 2]

Solution:
$C_2 = \sqrt{2 \times (h_1 - h_2) \times 1000} = \sqrt{2 \times (2800 - 2250) \times 1000} = 1048.8 \text{ m/s}$.[cite: 2]
From continuity equation, $A_2 = \frac{\dot{m} v_2}{C_2} = \frac{14 \times 1.25}{1048.8} = 0.01668 \text{ m}^2$.[cite: 2]

4. Condenser

Reverse of a boiler; removes heat to condense vapor. No work ($W=0$), neglected kinetic/potential energy.[cite: 2]
SFEE reduces to: $Q = \dot{m}(h_2 - h_1)$.[cite: 2]

Example: Condenser Heat Loss

Fluid enters at 35 kg/min with $h_1 = 2200 \text{ kJ/kg}$ and leaves with $h_2 = 255 \text{ kJ/kg}$. Find heat energy loss.[cite: 2]

Solution:
$Q = \dot{m}(h_2 - h_1) = 35 \times (255 - 2200) = -68,075 \text{ kJ/min}$.[cite: 2]

5. Throttle (Valve)

Fluid flows through a restriction causing a large pressure drop over a small distance. Insufficient area for heat loss ($Q=0$), no moving parts ($W=0$), neglected kinetic/potential energy.[cite: 2]
SFEE reduces to: $h_1 = h_2$ (Isenthalpic process).[cite: 2]

Example: Throttling Process

Fluid throttled from 10 bar to 1 bar. Specific volume changes from $0.3 \text{ m}^3/\text{kg}$ to $1.8 \text{ m}^3/\text{kg}$. Determine change in internal energy.[cite: 2]

Solution:
$h_1 = h_2 \rightarrow u_1 + P_1 v_1 = u_2 + P_2 v_2$.
$u_2 - u_1 = P_1 v_1 - P_2 v_2 = (1000 \times 0.3) - (100 \times 1.8) = 300 - 180 = 120 \text{ kJ/kg}$.[cite: 2]

6. Pump

The action of a pump is the reverse of a turbine; it uses external work energy to produce a pressure rise. $Q=0$, neglected kinetic/potential energy.[cite: 2]
SFEE reduces to: $-W = \dot{m}(h_2 - h_1)$. Since $h_2 > h_1$, $W$ is negative.[cite: 2]

Example: Pump Power

Pump delivers 45 kg/min. Inlet $h_1 = 46 \text{ kJ/kg}$, outlet $h_2 = 175 \text{ kJ/kg}$. Heat lost = 105 kJ/min. Drive efficiency = 85%. Find drive power.[cite: 2]

Solution:
$\dot{m} = 45 / 60 = 0.75 \text{ kg/s}$. $\dot{Q}_{\text{loss}} = -105 / 60 = -1.75 \text{ kW}$.[cite: 2]
$Q - W = \dot{m}(h_2 - h_1) \rightarrow -1.75 - W = 0.75(175 - 46) = 96.75$.
$W = -98.5 \text{ kW}$ (Work done ON fluid).[cite: 2]
Power required = $98.5 / 0.85 = 115.88 \text{ kW}$.[cite: 2]
