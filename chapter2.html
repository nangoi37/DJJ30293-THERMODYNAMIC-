Chapter 2: Properties of Pure Substances

2.1 Three Phases of Pure Substances

A pure substance can exist in three primary phases, defined by their molecular arrangement:

  • Solid Phase: Molecules are closely bound, relatively dense, and arranged in a rigid three-dimensional pattern. They do not easily deform (e.g., ice).
  • Liquid Phase: Molecules are closely bound and dense, but are no longer rigidly structured. They are free to move within a fixed volume (e.g., water).
  • Steam/Gas Phase: Molecules virtually do not attract each other, are spaced far apart, and move at random. There is neither a fixed volume nor a fixed shape (e.g., steam).

2.2 Phase Change Process at Constant Pressure

When heating water in a frictionless piston-cylinder at a constant pressure, the substance undergoes several distinct states:

  1. Compressed (Subcooled) Liquid: The fluid remains entirely within the liquid state, below the saturation temperature.
  2. Saturated Liquid: All fluid is liquid, but even the slightest addition of energy will result in the formation of some vapor.
  3. Saturated Liquid-Vapour Mixture (Wet Steam): Liquid and steam exist together in a mixture. As long as liquid is present, vaporization continues at a constant temperature.
  4. Saturated Steam: All fluid is in the vapor state, but the slightest loss of energy would result in condensation.
  5. Superheated Steam: The further addition of heat causes the temperature of the steam to increase at constant pressure.

2.3 Property Diagrams (T-v, P-v, P-T)

The variations of properties during phase-change processes are best studied using property diagrams:

  • T-v Diagram: Shows temperature versus specific volume. The constant pressure lines slope upwards in the compressed liquid region, are completely flat (horizontal) inside the saturated vapor dome, and curve upwards again in the superheated region.
  • P-v Diagram: Shows pressure versus specific volume. Constant temperature lines curve downwards in the superheated region and are completely flat inside the saturated vapor dome.
  • P-T Diagram: Often called the Phase Diagram. It shows the solid, liquid, and vapor regions separated by the sublimation, melting, and vaporization lines. The point where all three phases meet is the Triple point. The point where the liquid and vapor phases merge is the Critical point.

2.4 Thermodynamic Properties and Tables

Thermodynamic properties are tabulated for saturated liquid, saturated vapour, superheated vapor, and compressed liquid regions. Key properties include:

  • Enthalpy (H): Equal to the sum of internal energy plus the product of pressure and volume.
    H = U + PV
  • Internal Energy (u): The total kinetic and potential energy associated with molecular motion, including energy in chemical bonds.
  • Entropy (s): A property associated with the second law of thermodynamics, representing the measure of a system's molecular disorder or randomness.

In property tables, subscripts denote the state:

  • f = saturated liquid (e.g., vf)
  • g = saturated vapor (e.g., vg)
  • fg = the difference between saturated vapor and saturated liquid (e.g., vfg = vg - vf)

2.5 Properties of a Wet Mixture (Dryness Fraction)

During vaporization, the mixture is analyzed using the Dryness Fraction (Quality), x, which is the ratio of the mass of vapor to the total mass of the mixture:

x = msteam / mtotal
  • For a saturated liquid, x = 0.
  • For dry saturated steam, x = 1.
  • In the wet steam region, 0 < x < 1.

Because the volume of liquid is usually negligibly small compared to steam, the specific volume is calculated as v = x vg. Other properties are calculated as follows:

Key Mixture Equations:
Specific Enthalpy: h = hf + x hfg

Specific Internal Energy: u = uf + x (ug - uf)

Specific Entropy: s = sf + x sfg

2.6 Superheated Vapour

Steam is superheated when its temperature is greater than the saturation temperature (ts) corresponding to its pressure. The degree of superheat is given by:

Degree of superheat = tsuperheat - tsaturation

If specific internal energy is not tabulated for high pressures (above 70 bar), it can be calculated using the relation: u = h - Pv.

2.7 Interpolation Methods

When an exact value is not listed in the steam tables, we use interpolation to find it based on surrounding tabulated values.

Single Interpolation

Used when one variable (e.g., pressure) falls between two tabulated rows.

Example: Determine the saturation temperature at 77 bar.
From Tables: At 75 bar, ts = 290.5 °C. At 80 bar, ts = 295 °C.
Equation: (ts - 290.5) / (77 - 75) = (295 - 290.5) / (80 - 75)
Solution: ts = 292.3 °C

Double Interpolation

Used in superheated tables when two properties (e.g., Temperature and Pressure) are not exactly tabulated. You must interpolate across the rows first to find intermediate values, and then interpolate vertically between those new values.

Example: Determine specific enthalpy of superheated steam at 25 bar and 320°C.
Step 1: Find h1 at 20 bar, 320°C. Interpolate between 300°C (h=3025) and 350°C (h=3138).
-> h1 = 3070.2 kJ/kg.
Step 2: Find h2 at 30 bar, 320°C. Interpolate between 300°C (h=2995) and 350°C (h=3117).
-> h2 = 3043.8 kJ/kg.
Step 3: Interpolate between 20 bar (h1) and 30 bar (h2) to find h at 25 bar.
-> h = 3057 kJ/kg.

2.8 Ideal Gas Model (Equation of State)

Two foundational laws describe the behavior of a perfect gas:

  • Boyle's Law: If Temperature (T) remains constant, volume is inversely proportional to pressure. P1 V1 = P2 V2.
  • Charles' Law: If Pressure (P) remains constant, volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature. V1 / T1 = V2 / T2.

Combining these yields the Ideal-Gas Equation of State:

P v = R T   or   P V = m R T

Where P is absolute pressure (kPa), T is absolute temperature (K), v is specific volume (m³/kg), and R is the gas constant (kJ/kg K).

The gas constant R is determined from R = Ru / M, where Ru is the Universal Gas Constant (8.3144 kJ/kmol K) and M is the molecular weight.

2.9 Specific Heats

Specific heat is defined as the energy required to raise the temperature of a unit mass of a substance by one degree.

  • Specific Heat at Constant Volume (Cv): The heat supplied while volume remains constant.
    Heat flow equation: Q = m Cv (T2 - T1). This equals the change in internal energy (U2 - U1).
  • Specific Heat at Constant Pressure (Cp): The heat supplied while pressure remains constant.
    Heat flow equation: Q = m Cp (T2 - T1).
Relationship Between Specific Heats:
R = Cp - Cv
Specific Heat Ratio (γ) = Cp / Cv
