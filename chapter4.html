Chapter 4: Second Law of Thermodynamics

4.1 The First and Second Laws

The First Law states that the net heat supplied in a cycle is equal to the net work done. However, the Second Law indicates that in any complete cycle, the gross heat supplied must be greater than the work done; some heat must always be rejected by the system.

$$W = Q_1 - Q_2$$

4.2 Forward Heat Engines

Work can easily be converted to other forms of energy, but converting heat to work requires special devices called heat engines.

Figure 4.1: Schematic of a Steam Power Plant (Forward Heat Engine).
Kelvin-Planck Statement:
It is impossible for any device that operates on a cycle to receive heat from a single reservoir and produce a net amount of work.
No heat engine can have a thermal efficiency of 100 percent.
Figure 4.2: Abstract Schematic of a Forward Heat Engine.

Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{th}$)

The fraction of heat input converted to net work output is the thermal efficiency.

$$\eta_{th} = \frac{W_{\text{net,out}}}{Q_{\text{in}}} = \frac{Q_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}}}{Q_{\text{in}}} = 1 - \frac{Q_{\text{out}}}{Q_{\text{in}}}$$

Example: Power Plant Thermal Efficiency

A steam power plant receives heat from a furnace at a rate of 280 GJ/h. Heat losses to the surrounding air from the pipes are 8 GJ/h. Waste heat is transferred to the cooling water at 145 GJ/h. Determine net power output and thermal efficiency.

Solution:
Total heat rejected ($Q_{\text{out}}$): $145 + 8 = 153 \text{ GJ/h}$.
Cycle Net Work ($W_{\text{net}}$): $Q_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}} = 280 - 153 = 127 \text{ GJ/h}$.
Convert to MW: $127 \times \frac{10^3 \text{ MJ}}{3600 \text{ s}} = 35.3 \text{ MW}$.
Cycle Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{th}$): $\frac{127}{280} = 0.454 \text{ (or } 45.4\%)$.

Example: Car Engine Fuel Consumption

A car engine with a power output of 48.5 kW has a thermal efficiency of 24%. The fuel has a heating value of 9092 kJ/kg. Determine the fuel consumption rate.

Solution:
Required Heat Input ($Q_{\text{in}}$): $\frac{W_{\text{net}}}{\eta_{th}} = \frac{48.5}{0.24} = 202.083 \text{ kW (kJ/s)}$.
Fuel Consumption Rate ($\dot{m}$): $\frac{Q_{\text{in}}}{\text{Heating Value}} = \frac{202.083}{9092} = 0.022 \text{ kg/s}$.

4.3 Reverse Heat Engines (Refrigerators & Heat Pumps)

A reversed heat engine (like a Refrigerator or Heat Pump) pumps heat from a low-temperature region to a high-temperature region by utilizing work input.

Figure 4.3: Schematic of a Reverse Heat Engine (Refrigerator / Heat Pump).
Clausius Statement:
No device can transfer heat from a cooler body to a warmer one without leaving an effect on the surroundings.
A refrigerator cannot operate unless driven by an external power source.
Refrigerator: $$COP_R = \frac{Q_L}{W_{\text{net,in}}}$$
Heat Pump: $$COP_{HP} = \frac{Q_H}{W_{\text{net,in}}}$$

Example: Refrigerator COP (Reverse Cycle)

A household refrigerator with a COP of 1.2 removes heat from the refrigerated space at a rate of 60 kJ/min. Determine the electric power consumed and heat transferred to the kitchen air.

Solution:
Convert $Q_L$: $60 \text{ kJ/min} \times \left(\frac{1 \text{ min}}{60 \text{ s}}\right) = 1.0 \text{ kW}$.
Power Consumed ($W_{\text{in}}$): $\frac{Q_L}{COP_R} = \frac{1.0}{1.2} = 0.83 \text{ kW}$.
Heat to Kitchen ($Q_H$): $W_{\text{in}} + Q_L = 0.83 + 1.0 = 1.83 \text{ kW}$.

Example: Heat Pump Performance

Determine the COP of a heat pump that supplies energy to a house at a rate of 8000 kJ/h for each kW of electric power it draws. Also, determine the rate of energy absorption from outdoor air.

Solution:
Convert $W_{\text{in}}$ to match units: $1 \text{ kW} = 3600 \text{ kJ/h}$.
$COP_{HP} = \frac{Q_H}{W_{\text{in}}} = \frac{8000}{3600} = 2.22$.
Convert $Q_H$ to kW: $8000 / 3600 = 2.22 \text{ kW}$.
Energy Absorption ($Q_L$): $Q_H - W_{\text{in}} = 2.22 - 1.0 = 1.22 \text{ kW}$.

4.4 Vapor Power Cycles

Carnot Vapor Cycle

The Carnot cycle is the most efficient cycle operating between two specified temperature limits, but it is not a suitable model for actual power cycles due to practical limitations.

Figure 4.4: T-s Diagram of a Carnot Vapor Cycle.
  • Process 4-1: Isothermal heat addition in a boiler $\rightarrow Q_{in} = h_1 - h_4$.
  • Process 1-2: Isentropic expansion in a turbine $\rightarrow W_T = h_1 - h_2$.
  • Process 2-3: Isothermal heat rejection in a condenser $\rightarrow Q_{out} = h_2 - h_3$.
  • Process 3-4: Isentropic compression in a compressor $\rightarrow W_C = h_4 - h_3$.
$$\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_2}{T_1}$$

Rankine Cycle (Ideal Cycle for Vapor Power Plants)

Many impracticalities of the Carnot cycle are eliminated by superheating the steam in the boiler and condensing it completely in the condenser.

Figure 4.5: T-s Diagram of an Ideal Rankine Cycle.
  • Process 4-1: Constant pressure heat addition in a boiler.
  • Process 1-2: Isentropic expansion in a turbine.
  • Process 2-3: Constant pressure heat rejection in a condenser.
  • Process 3-4: Isentropic compression in a pump.
Rankine Cycle Equations:
$$Q_{\text{in}} = h_1 - h_4 \quad \mid \quad Q_{\text{out}} = h_2 - h_3$$
$$W_{\text{Turbine}} = h_1 - h_2 \quad \mid \quad W_{\text{Pump}} = h_4 - h_3 = v_f(P_4 - P_3)$$
$$W_{\text{net}} = W_T - W_P = Q_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}}$$
